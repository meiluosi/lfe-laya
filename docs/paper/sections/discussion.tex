\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The value of a frozen prior is conditional.}
A caveat applies to the growth of the prior's value with the delay: at the longest delay most no-prior genomes behaved like a blind eater (Section~\ref{sec:res-delay}), so the learner without a prior was effectively absent there. In the pre-registered comparisons the prior helped when consequences were delayed and its benefit grew with the delay (Section~\ref{sec:res-delay}); it was not harmful, but not demonstrably useful either, when feedback was immediate; and it added nothing when experience-only individuals could already solve the task (Section~\ref{sec:res-fair}). The same pattern explains why a seemingly large early effect did not replicate (Section~\ref{sec:res-null}) and why an apparent harm without delay appeared under mismatched training (Section~\ref{sec:res-delay}). We therefore read the prior as a way to avoid costly experiments, whose worth rises with the cost of an experiment: a delayed consequence means that the creature cannot learn from one meal which food made it ill, so a trusted prior replaces trial and error that would otherwise be expensive or fatal.

\paragraph{What evolution learns is how much to trust.}
The genomes do not contain the knowledge in the prior; they contain how strongly to rely on it and how to combine it with experience. This is why the trust learned for the frozen model carried over to a dialect it had not been trained on, whereas the trust learned for a sentiment lexicon did not (Section~\ref{sec:res-lexicon}): the lexicon's reliability depends on the dialect, the frozen model's did not in our experiments. We have not tested this explanation directly, and the lexicon's disadvantage disappears when the text can be read by sentiment alone (Section~\ref{sec:res-cues}).

\paragraph{Cues are used, but the manipulation must be read with care.}
Individuals with a prior gained from true neighbour appearances and true stage phrases and lost when they were mismatched, while individuals without a prior ignored them. A mismatch, however, relabels a cue consistently; individuals whose beliefs are keyed on the literal text are unaffected by it, which is why the cost of a mismatch measures reliance on the \emph{meaning} of the cue rather than on the cue as such.

\paragraph{Implications.}
For a designer of agents that combine a pretrained model with learning, the practical message is conditional: use a frozen prior when experimentation is costly, delayed or dangerous and when the model's judgements are reliable for the text at hand; do not expect it to help in worlds where trial and error suffices; and do not take an advantage over a sentiment lexicon as evidence of understanding unless the texts require knowledge that sentiment does not carry.

\paragraph{Relation to the Baldwin effect.}
Our design separates the genome from the frozen model and allows the contribution of each to be varied, which is the setting in which one could ask whether evolution can assimilate what a frozen model knows. We have run only a development-phase experiment on this question and do not draw conclusions here.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Simulation and templated scenarios.} The world is a simulated grid with templated food scenarios; the results concern the behaviour of evolved agents in this ecology, not real organisms or open-ended language.
\item \textbf{Author-written dialects.} All four dialects were written by one author. They are independent of any model output, but they are still one person's natural language; the factual dialect in particular encodes the author's idea of ``world knowledge''.
\item \textbf{Hand-tuned world difficulty and thresholds.} Effect-size thresholds were set from development-phase results, and the difficulty of the ripening world was adjusted during development. This may favour individuals that have a prior. Two comparisons (pre-registrations 04 and 08) show a small effect in a certain direction that missed its threshold; the tiered reading in Table~\ref{tab:tiers} makes this visible.
\item \textbf{Structured individuals.} Individuals are a rule skeleton with nine or ten evolved parameters; the prior enters through one term of a belief update. Results may differ for other individuals, including neural policies.
\item \textbf{The frozen model is used as a cache.} \laya{} is not called in the simulation loop, and it is an encoder with a fine-tuned decision head rather than a generative language model; we do not claim that the findings extend to generative models. Its model card describes it as a base to be specialised, and we observe high zero-shot discrimination only for our food-description task, after averaging over two option orders to remove its preference for the first option.
\item \textbf{Training seeds vary only the evolutionary noise.} The sequence of 240 training worlds (six per generation over 40 generations) is the same for every evolutionary run; a training seed changes only the random perturbations of the evolution strategy. Statements such as ``eight of eight seeds agree'' therefore support the claim that evolutionary noise does not change the conclusion \emph{on this sequence of training worlds}; they do not show that a different draw of training worlds would give the same result. We plan to let the training worlds vary with the seed in later experiments.
\item \textbf{The experience-only learner fails at long delays.} At delay 80, six of eight no-prior genomes were indistinguishable from the blind rule, which shows that the credit window we use cannot assign a delayed shock to the right meal when several foods are eaten within the window. This is the main way in which the conclusion about delay is relative to the learner (next item).
\item \textbf{The strength of the experience-only learner is not varied.} Individuals without a prior learn from experience with a simple average belief and a credit window for delayed consequences. A stronger learner (for example, with eligibility traces, a bias toward blaming novel foods, or posterior sampling) might narrow the advantage of the prior in delayed worlds. The conclusion that the prior's value grows with the delay is therefore relative to the learner used here.
\item \textbf{Learnability and the cost of experiments are not separated.} The world in which experience suffices ($K=4$) differs from the hard ripening world ($K=16$) in a single parameter that changes both learnability and how much a cue can add; we have two points, not a dose--response curve. Likewise, the explanation that a prior helps by avoiding costly experiments is not tested, because delay and lethality were not manipulated separately.
\item \textbf{Weak baselines for the frozen model.} The comparisons against a lexicon use an unadjusted sentiment lexicon and an author-written one; we did not evaluate any other pretrained model, so we cannot separate the contribution of ``a pretrained model'' from that of this particular checkpoint. The data on which the decision head of \laya{} was fine-tuned are not known to us, and the dialects are template-generated from small clause banks, so the ability we measure is best described as generalisation to unseen phrasing rather than understanding of meaning.
\item \textbf{Fitness weights and training budget.} Fitness weights survival and final energy as $0.7$ and $0.3$ without a sensitivity analysis, and individuals are evolved for 40 generations with a population of 24; we do not show that every condition has converged.
\item \textbf{Time stamps of the pre-registrations.} They are the commit times of a repository controlled by the author; an external reader cannot verify that history has not been rewritten until the hashes are deposited with an independent time-stamping service.
\item \textbf{Small numbers of seeds and rough intervals.} Most comparisons use six or eight training seeds; intervals over seeds are rough. The exact sign-flip test can reach $p=1/256$ at best with eight seeds.
\item \textbf{Reuse of evaluation pools and multiple testing.} The held-out field-notes pool was evaluated in several pre-registrations (summary statistics only, never used for tuning), and hypothesis tests are not corrected across pre-registrations.
\item \textbf{$\texttt{laya}-\texttt{none}$ is not a fair learning comparison.} An individual without a prior cannot read the descriptions at all; in two ripening worlds most of them evolved never to eat. The comparison that treats both sides fairly is the one in Section~\ref{sec:res-fair}.
\item \textbf{Procedural deviations.} They are listed in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.
\item \textbf{Throughput.} Our measurements do not support extrapolation to larger populations or to calling a large model at every decision.
\end{itemize}
\section{Reproducibility and data}
All code, the four dialects and their generated pools, the cached model judgements and embeddings, the evolved genomes with their hashes, and the raw results of every pre-registered evaluation are in the project repository (code under Apache-2.0, text and data under CC BY 4.0). The evaluation scripts refuse to run if a genome hash differs from the committed one, and the cached judgements make every evaluation reproducible without running the model.
