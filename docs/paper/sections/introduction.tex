\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Suppose a creature must decide what to eat from a short description of each food, in a world where a wrong choice may be fatal and where the harm can appear long after the meal. Three sources of competence could help. The creature's \emph{innate} parameters, shaped by evolution, decide how cautious or curious it is. Its \emph{acquired} knowledge, built from its own experience, tells it which foods it has found harmful. And a pretrained model can supply a prior judgement about the description itself, before the creature has eaten anything: ``a pale root growing on the slag heap behind the old smelter'' sounds dangerous to a reader who knows what smelters leave behind. How much each source contributes, and when, is hard to say in general, because in most simple environments one of them already suffices.

This paper studies the case in which the pretrained model is \emph{frozen}: it is never updated, evolution changes only the genomes of the individuals, and the question is how much those genomes should trust the model's judgements. We build a controlled ecology in which the three sources can be separated, and we ask under which conditions a frozen model's prior helps individuals whose decision parameters are evolved and whose beliefs are learned within a lifetime. Because the answer turned out to depend on details of the world, we pre-registered every confirmatory comparison, evolved 500 distinct genomes under a hash-frozen protocol, and report positive, null and negative results alike.

Our contributions are as follows. (1)~A testbed with a generative ontology and four author-written dialects, delayed and deceptive consequences, language cues that change over time, and social observation, in which innate, acquired and frozen-model contributions can be separated; and a pre-registration workflow for evolutionary experiments with a tiered reading of effect sizes. (2)~Evidence that the value of the prior is conditional and that a large part of its measured advantage at long delays reflects a weak experience-only learner: with a credit-assignment learner that is blind to delay, the prior's advantage is between $+0.015$ and $+0.05$ at 40 ticks and between $+0.05$ and $+0.09$ at 80 ticks (positive in every seed at 80 ticks), but most genomes without a prior then behave like a blind eater; with a delay-aware learner whose lag evolution finds by itself, the advantage at 80 ticks falls to about $+0.02$ (interval including zero) and disappears at 40 ticks. (3)~Evidence that the frozen model beats a sentiment lexicon only where descriptions require world knowledge, and that the trust learned for the frozen model transfers across dialects while the trust learned for a lexicon does not. (4)~Evidence that individuals use language cues from neighbours and from ripening stages, and lose when the cues are wrong. (5)~A distilled linear read-out that can replace the frozen model on one dialect but not on a dialect that needs world knowledge. (6)~Several null and negative results, including that an early large effect did not replicate across training seeds.

We use the term ``prior'' for a cached judgement that individuals read; the frozen model is not called in the simulation loop, and our results concern this use, not a model that acts. The experiments are simulations with templated food scenarios written by one author; we discuss what this does and does not support in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. The rest of the paper reviews related work (Section~\ref{sec:related}), describes the ecology, the frozen model and the individuals (Section~\ref{sec:methods}) and the evaluation protocol (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), reports the results (Section~\ref{sec:results}), and discusses their implications and limits.
